Wheeled Biped - V1

July 2024 — August 2024 • Personal Project • First Iteration

My long-term goal in building these robots was to eventually tackle the challenge of a humanoid biped. Knowing the difficulty of bipedal locomotion, I saw a wheeled biped as a useful intermediate step. Wheels simplified locomotion while preserving the challenges of articulated legs and dynamic balance.

I began V1 toward the end of the summer term. Having relied primarily on hobby servos in previous robots, I wanted to better understand the motors and transmissions driving each joint. Inspired by Stanley , a rope-driven quadruped, I decided to explore capstan drives as a potential low-reduction, near-zero-backlash transmission.

The broader wheeled-biped engineering carries over into V2 and my capstone project, where I explore leg optimization, dynamic modeling, actuator sizing, and system integration.

This page instead focuses on robotic actuator design, capstan mechanics, and the trade-offs that led me away from rope-driven transmissions.

1. Actuator Selection

1.1 What Makes a Good Actuator?

For V1, I wanted to understand what makes an actuator suitable for a dynamic robot.

The main considerations were torque density, speed, efficiency, backdrivability, and reliability. Ideally, an actuator would produce high joint torque while introducing minimal resistance to external forces. In practice, these characteristics compete, particularly when selecting the transmission ratio.

1.2 Reduction Ratio and Reflected Inertia

Electric motors generally operate at higher speeds and lower torques than robotic joints require. A gearbox trades motor speed for output torque:

\[\omega_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{\omega_m}{N},\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{out}}\approx\eta N\tau_m\]

N = reduction ratio; η = forward transmission efficiency.

The less obvious consequence of increasing reduction is reflected inertia : the motor's rotational inertia as experienced at the output joint.

\[J_{\mathrm{reflected}}=N^2J_m\]

J m = motor rotor inertia; ideal rigid transmission.

Doubling the reduction ratio quadruples the reflected motor inertia. While higher reduction allows smaller motors to generate greater joint torque, it also increases resistance to external acceleration. This is particularly undesirable for joints that need to respond quickly to impacts or changes in loading.

1.3 Backdrivability and Transparency

Two characteristics I became interested in were backdrivability and transparency .

Backdrivability is the ability of an external joint torque to drive the motor backward through the transmission. Transparency describes how little the actuator's own inertia, friction, backlash, and compliance interfere with external forces.

For an ideal, unpowered rigid transmission:

\[\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}=\left(J_L+N^2J_m\right)\ddot{\theta}\]

J L = load inertia; θ̈ = joint angular acceleration.

Real transmissions introduce additional resistance through friction, stiction, and other losses.

An important distinction is that efficiency and transparency are not equivalent . Even a hypothetical 100%-efficient gearbox retains its reflected inertia. Real gearbox efficiency is also nonlinear, varying with torque, speed, and direction of motion.

These effects contribute to the actuator's apparent resistance to acceleration. Friction and control behaviour can make this resistance vary with operating conditions, rather than behaving like a constant inertia.

For a dynamic robot, I wanted joints that could respond to external disturbances without excessive mechanical resistance. Higher transparency also makes proprioceptive force estimation more practical, since motor current and joint measurements provide a more predictable indication of external loading when transmission losses are small.

1.4 Quasi-Direct Drive

This led me toward quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuators: high-torque motors paired with relatively low reduction ratios.

Lower reduction limits reflected inertia and can reduce friction and backlash, improving backdrivability and torque control. These characteristics are particularly useful for a balancing robot, where the wheels and legs must continuously respond to ground forces and disturbances.

The ideal would be a compact, high-torque actuator with minimal parasitic driving and backdriving resistance, high stiffness, and negligible backlash.

However, low reduction also means greater motor torque and current requirements. High torque density can come at the expense of continuous performance when thermal management is insufficient. Sealing, lubrication, and bearing design introduce further compromises between mechanical resistance, packaging, and long-term reliability.

QDD offered a promising direction, particularly for its low reflected inertia and potential for backdrivability. For V1, I chose to explore a capstan-based QDD actuator, hoping to achieve these characteristics through a low-ratio, near-zero-backlash rope transmission.

2. Capstan Drive Mechanics

A capstan drive transmits torque through tensioned rope wrapped around rotating drums. Without meshing gear teeth, it offers the potential for minimal backlash and good backdrivability. However, its performance depends on rope tension, friction, stiffness, and mechanical design.

2.1 Torque Transmission and the Capstan Effect

Capstan drives rely on friction between the rope and drum to transmit torque. The difference in rope tension produces torque about the drum axis:

\[\tau=(T_{\mathrm{high}}-T_{\mathrm{low}})r\]

\(T_{\mathrm{high}}\), \(T_{\mathrm{low}}\) = rope tensions; \(r\) = effective drum radius.

The maximum tension ratio before slipping is governed by the capstan equation :

\[\frac{T_{\mathrm{high}}}{T_{\mathrm{low}}}\leq e^{\mu\theta}\]

\(\mu\) = rope–drum friction coefficient; \(\theta\) = wrap angle in radians.

Increasing wrap angle exponentially increases the available tension ratio, while pretension helps maintain engagement and sufficient tension under changing loads.

For a wheeled biped, frequent torque reversals make maintaining tension particularly important. Insufficient pretension risks slack or slip, while excessive pretension increases bearing loads, cable stress, and wear. Higher cable loads may also require thicker rope, affecting bending fatigue, terminations, and packaging.

2.2 Reduction Ratio and Mechanical Design

The capstan equation describes frictional traction, not the reduction ratio. Assuming no slip, the effective drum radii determine the reduction:

\[N=\frac{r_{\mathrm{out}}}{r_{\mathrm{in}}}\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{out}}\approx\eta N\tau_{\mathrm{in}}\]

\(r_{\mathrm{in}}\), \(r_{\mathrm{out}}\) = effective drum radii; \(\eta\) = forward efficiency.

For V1, I considered a reduction of approximately 8:1, consistent with the QDD approach.

A key packaging constraint was the D/d ratio (drum diameter to cable diameter). Thicker cables generally require larger minimum bending diameters to limit fatigue and maintain service life.

This limits the minimum input drum diameter. At a fixed reduction ratio, a larger input drum requires a proportionally larger output drum, quickly increasing the transmission's footprint.

The rope also required sufficient wrap angle, secure terminations, and a tensioning mechanism. My configuration used separate input and output axes, further complicating packaging within the leg.

2.3 Backlash, Compliance, and Transparency

The main attraction of capstan drives was their potential for near-zero geometric backlash. With sufficient pretension, the rope remains engaged without the clearance associated with meshing gear teeth.

However, low backlash does not necessarily mean high stiffness or transparency .

Synthetic cables such as Dyneema and Vectran exhibit compliance, hysteresis, and varying degrees of creep. Their effective stiffness depends on braid construction, pretension, and loading history, making simple linear elastic models unreliable without experimental characterization.

Rope stretch allows relative displacement between the motor and joint, affecting position accuracy and torque transmission. Friction from rope bending and microslip introduces additional losses and direction-dependent behavior.

For a balancing robot, I wanted predictable joint responses to external forces. Low backlash was attractive, but compliance and friction complicated the goal of high transparency.

2.4 Compliance and Control Bandwidth

Rope compliance also affects the actuator's dynamic response. Rather than behaving as a single rigid inertia, the motor and load form a two-inertia system coupled by the rope's effective torsional stiffness.

For an ideal, undamped model with inertias and stiffness referred to the same shaft:

\[\omega_{\mathrm{ar}}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{J_L}}\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{r}}=\sqrt{k\left(\frac{1}{J_m}+\frac{1}{J_L}\right)}\]

\(k\) = effective torsional stiffness; \(J_m\), \(J_L\) = motor and load inertias referred to the same shaft.

Lower rope stiffness reduces these frequencies, bringing flexible dynamics closer to the intended control bandwidth. Near resonance, the transmission can amplify oscillations and introduce phase changes that complicate feedback control.

For a wheeled biped, where actuators must respond quickly to disturbances, these flexible dynamics can limit achievable control bandwidth. Some compliance is useful for absorbing impacts, but uncontrolled elasticity makes precise torque and position control more difficult.

2.5 Reliability and Long-Term Performance

Maintaining consistent transmission behavior also presents practical challenges.

Rope creep and relaxation can alter pretension and stiffness over time, while repeated loading may introduce wear and changes in friction. Terminations must securely hold the cable without slipping or creating excessive stress concentrations.

These effects can require periodic retensioning or recalibration, adding maintenance and mechanical complexity.

Ultimately, the challenge was not simply achieving a low-backlash reduction, but maintaining stiffness, transparency, and predictable torque transmission in a compact, reliable actuator.

3. V1 Design and Implementation

For V1, I estimated joint torque requirements and reduction ratios by benchmarking actuator specifications from other wheeled bipeds. With limited development time, this provided a practical starting point, but it did not account for differences in leg geometry, mass distribution, or dynamic loading.

A more rigorous approach involves modeling the robot's kinematics and dynamics to establish joint torque - speed requirements, then selecting motors and transmissions based on peak and continuous performance, thermal limits, and reflected inertia. I explore this process in greater depth in V2 and my capstone project .

I ultimately built a functioning leg incorporating the capstan transmissions. With the school year approaching, I disassembled the prototype and shifted my focus toward learning about motor control strategies and planning for the next biped iteration.

4. Why I Moved Away from Capstan Drives

Although promising in theory, integrating the capstan transmission into a functioning leg revealed several practical challenges:

  • Pretension: Difficult to maintain consistent rope tension during assembly and adjustment.
  • Packaging: Large drums, cable thickness, and precise drum spacing complicated the design.
  • System complexity: Rope routing, terminations, and tensioning hardware added assembly challenges.
  • Dynamic performance: Compliance, friction, and hysteresis raised concerns about transparency and control bandwidth, although these effects were not experimentally characterized.

In retrospect, V1 reinforced the importance of evaluating an actuator as a complete system. Backlash, efficiency, backdrivability, stiffness, and transparency are distinct characteristics; improving one does not necessarily improve the others.

While I ultimately moved away from capstan drives, the capstan effect remains useful in other applications, particularly cable-driven robotic hands, where friction can secure terminations but complicate precise force transmission.

For V2, I moved toward a more conventional actuator architecture while retaining the same priorities: sufficient torque, low reflected inertia, good backdrivability, and predictable force transmission. V2 builds on these lessons through leg optimization, dynamic modeling, and systematic actuator sizing, with further development continuing into my capstone project.
