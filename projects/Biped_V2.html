Wheeled Biped - V2

January 2025 — March 2025 • Personal Project • Second Iteration

Following V1, I moved away from capstan drives to develop a second wheeled biped focused on compact leg design and actuator integration. My wave drive actuator and V2's leg architecture were developed in parallel, with each influencing the other's design.

This page focuses on V2's mechanical development, particularly its belt-driven transmission. More extensive discussions of leg kinematics, dynamic modeling, actuator sizing, and control are covered in my capstone project .

1. Leg Architecture and Actuator Placement

For a 2-DOF leg, actuator placement influences mass distribution, joint inertia, transmission design, and the space available for bearings and structural components. I compared several ways of arranging the actuators while preserving the required leg motion.

Arrangement Advantages Trade-offs
Serial Simple joint layout; direct actuation Distal motor mass increases leg inertia and proximal joint loading
Opposing Co-locates motors near the hip without concentric shafts Requires axial clearance and compatible linkage geometry
Concentric / Stacked Coaxial joint outputs; compact planar footprint More involved shaft, bearing, and axial packaging
Side-by-Side + Belts Flexible motor placement; remote torque transmission Additional pulleys, belt tensioning, and compliance

Decision: I chose a motor arrangement with belt transmissions , primarily to minimize the leg's axial width. This was important for the biped's overall appearance, allowing the legs to sit farther apart relative to the body width without making the robot unnecessarily wide.

Using belts also gave me more flexibility in actuator placement, avoiding the need for stacked motors or concentric shafts. However, this decision introduced a new set of design considerations, including belt selection, pulley sizing, alignment, and tensioning, which became a significant part of V2's development.

2. Belt and Pulley Design

2.1 Transmission Selection

I initially considered using rigid linkages with spherical joints to transfer motion between the offset actuators and leg joints. While mechanically simple, their geometry restricted the available range of motion, and I struggled to package two linkage rods within the leg without interference.

I ultimately chose synchronous belt transmissions for their flexibility in routing power between offset shafts. Changing pulley sizes also allowed me to adjust the reduction ratio if my initial actuator sizing proved inaccurate. Seeing belt transmissions used in other quadrupeds (especially MIT cheetah) provided a useful design reference, and I was interested in gaining practical experience with belt selection, pulley design, and tensioning.

2.2 Estimating Joint Requirements

To estimate the required joint torque and speed, I referenced several existing legged robot designs, particularly the MIT Mini Cheetah and Michael Taylor's series elastic actuator study. These provided useful reference points for actuator performance and joint loading. Unlike these more dynamically demanding designs, V2 was not intended for jumping or highly dynamic legged locomotion, allowing me to target a less demanding operating envelope.

Using Taylor's published maximum human knee torque of 360 N·m, I scaled the requirement to an estimated robot mass of 6.8 kg and two 200 mm leg links. Assuming comparable loading relative to body weight and leg length:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\mathrm{est}} &= \tau_{\mathrm{ref}} \frac{m_{\mathrm{V2}}}{m_{\mathrm{ref}}} \frac{L_{\mathrm{V2}}}{L_{\mathrm{ref}}} \\[6pt] &= 360 \left(\frac{6.8}{80}\right) \left(\frac{0.4}{1.0}\right) \\[6pt] &= \boxed{12.24\ \mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}} \end{aligned} \]

Torque scaled by mass and characteristic leg length; approximate similarity assumption.

I used this as a preliminary torque benchmark for both joints, with a slightly higher reduction at the knee to provide additional capacity for configurations where knee loading could be greater.

Based on an estimated motor torque of 1.616 N·m, the 8:1 wave actuator provided a theoretical output of 12.93 N·m. The additional belt reductions increased the available joint torque according to:

\[ \tau_{\mathrm{joint}} = \tau_m i_{\mathrm{wave}} i_{\mathrm{belt}} \]

Ideal transmission; efficiency losses neglected.

Parameter Thigh Knee
Belt Reduction 54/45 = 1.20:1 40/32 = 1.25:1
Total Reduction 9.6:1 10:1
Theoretical Joint Torque 15.51 N·m 16.16 N·m
Margin Above Benchmark 26.7% 32.0%

Both joints theoretically exceeded the preliminary torque benchmark. These calculations neglected transmission losses and were not verified through a complete dynamic model or torque-speed characterization.

2.3 Belt and Pulley Selection

I selected HTD 5M synchronous belts for their rounded tooth profile, positive engagement, and availability in compact sizes. The thigh used two parallel 6 mm belts, while the knee used one 9 mm belt.

To estimate the belt loads, I related the wave actuator's theoretical output torque to the driving pulley pitch radius:

\[ \Delta T = \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{wave}}}{r_p}, \qquad r_p = \frac{Np}{2\pi} \]

ΔT = tight-side minus slack-side tension; N = driver teeth; p = 5 mm belt pitch.

Using the theoretical wave actuator output of 12.93 N·m, I calculated the effective belt forces for each transmission:

Parameter Thigh Knee
Driver / Driven Teeth 45 / 54 32 / 40
Driver Pitch Radius 35.81 mm 25.46 mm
Total Effective Belt Force 361 N 508 N
Belt Arrangement 2 × 6 mm 1 × 9 mm
Effective Force Per Belt 181 N (equal sharing) 508 N

The calculated forces provided a basis for comparing belt widths. Manufacturer design guidance, such as the Gates synchronous belt design manual, evaluates belt capacity using pulley geometry, operating speed, belt width, and tooth engagement. Increasing belt width generally increases the available load capacity, but also requires more axial space.

While the selected belts were expected to accommodate the intended loading, their capacity was not rigorously validated against manufacturer power ratings and service factors. This remained a limitation of the preliminary transmission design.

2.4 Parallel Belt Arrangement

Since the thigh and knee transmissions shared a common rotational axis, their belts had to be positioned at different axial locations. Placing one belt farther from its supporting bearings increased the bending moment on its shaft, creating a tradeoff between the two joints.

To reduce this offset, I split the thigh transmission into two parallel 6 mm belts, positioning the single 9 mm knee belt between them. This arrangement kept the knee belt centrally located while distributing the thigh's loading across both sides. I prioritized the knee because I expected it to experience greater joint loads.

In retrospect, this arrangement introduced more complexity than it justified. Unequal belt tension and alignment could cause the two thigh belts to carry different loads, reducing the benefit of splitting them. Combined with the additional tensioning challenges discussed in the next section, two conventional, side-by-side belt transmissions would likely have been a simpler and more reliable solution.

2.5 Belt Tensioning

One of the more challenging aspects of the transmission was developing a compact method of adjusting and maintaining belt tension. Insufficient pretension risks tooth skipping, while excessive pretension increases bearing loads and friction.

I referenced two existing designs ( here and here) that used a screw-adjusted roller mounted on a pivoting arm. This allowed me to adjust belt tension without repositioning the actuators.

Since the joints operate bidirectionally, the tight and slack belt spans alternate depending on the direction of torque. Although a single fixed idler can theoretically provide sufficient pretension, I found it difficult to maintain consistent tension in practice. Using tensioners on both spans would have provided greater adjustment flexibility.

Another issue was that the supplied belts were slightly longer than specified, making it harder to achieve the desired pretension. In retrospect, I also should have used independent tensioners for each parallel belt, allowing their tensions to be adjusted separately rather than relying on a shared mechanism.

3. Mechanical Development

I iterated on the leg assembly to integrate the actuators, pulleys, bearings, and structural components within the available space. Beyond the basic kinematics, the design had to accommodate belt routing, component clearances, and practical assembly access.

4. Retrospect

V2 reinforced that actuator packaging is a system-level design problem. Moving the motors can improve mass distribution or fit, but the transmission, bearings, tensioning hardware, and assembly process must be considered together. The belt-driven arrangement solved my immediate packaging constraint while introducing additional integration complexity.

The broader work on leg geometry, torque requirements, dynamics, and controls continues in my capstone project , where those decisions are developed more systematically.
