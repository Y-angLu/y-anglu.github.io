Concept Development & Design Decisions

Capstone / FYDP • September 2026 — Present • Notify Robots

From early sketches and ideation to the embedded wheeled-biped architecture, this is how Nudge’s design came together.

1. Where It All Started

What is Capstone?

Capstone, or our Final Year Design Project (FYDP), is a year-long engineering project where a team of students takes an idea from concept to a working prototype. It’s a chance to apply what we’ve learned to something we actually want to build.

Early Ideation

I think we knew pretty early on that we wanted to build something for the home. Home robotics is one of the most exciting spaces right now (in my opinion), largely because of the challenges of designing robots to live alongside people, especially human-robot interaction.

We wanted to build something that did a seemingly simple task, but did it well and with personality. A lot of this came from my time working around humanoid robots, and companies like Fauna and Sunday that put so much thought into how a robot looks, feels, and fits into people’s lives.

Utility and personality became the two driving principles behind Nudge.

Our first idea was a small autonomous robot that could push in chairs, close cabinets, and do other little things around the house. That was actually where the name Nudge came from. We eventually realized that safely manipulating heavy, unpredictable objects was a much bigger challenge than the utility justified.

Why Physical Notifications?

The name stuck, though, and eventually inspired the idea of physical notifications . Rather than moving objects, what if Nudge could move around the home and gently get someone’s attention? It was still a simple task, but one that felt more useful and gave us room to explore personality through movement.

I also liked that notifications didn’t always have to come from a screen or speaker. Nudge could come find someone, get their attention, and communicate through movement and touch. It felt like a fun way to explore human-robot interaction without needing a robot that could manipulate everything around the house.

The Birth of Notice

One issue was that Nudge alone would be very hardware-heavy, with relatively little software scope. Its small, soft design was important for safe physical interaction, but also limited its ability to see things like countertops and stovetops.

We initially considered a telescoping arm, but eventually realized the two responsibilities didn’t really belong on the same robot. Monitoring the home is relatively static, while delivering notifications is much more dynamic.

That’s how Notice came about: a taller, lightweight companion responsible for observing the environment, with Nudge acting as its mobile counterpart to relay information and interact with the user.

With their roles established, we could finally start thinking about what they should look like. Given our time constraints, Notice has taken a backseat as a simpler, caster-mounted tripod design. The rest of this page focuses on Nudge.

2. Choosing a Locomotion Architecture

The first big question was how Nudge was going to get around. We narrowed it down to four main options:

  • Wheeled base: Something like a Roomba or Matic.
  • Segway: A two-wheeled balancing robot.
  • Quadruped: With or without wheels.
  • Wheeled biped: Two wheels with articulated legs.

Why a Wheeled Biped?

Our concern with a conventional wheeled base or Segway was that they didn’t offer much in terms of character or expression. I wanted to avoid Nudge becoming just another appliance that happened to move around the house. We wanted people to interact with it as something with personality, rather than purely a tool.

The quadruped had almost the opposite problem. Four legs felt a little too animal-like for the character we were imagining. It also offered capabilities we didn’t really need, like greater load capacity, static stability, and obstacle traversal, at the cost of additional weight and complexity.

The wheeled biped felt like the right middle ground. Two wheels would provide efficient movement around the home, while the legs could give Nudge a sense of character through changes in height, posture, and movement.

It offered a simpler and lighter alternative to a quadruped without sacrificing the capabilities we actually cared about. Of course, having only two wheels meant we’d have to deal with balancing, but that was a trade-off we were willing to make.

3. The Nudging Mechanism

With the locomotion architecture decided, the nudging mechanism went through quite a few iterations.

My original idea was a multi-DOF, finger-like probe extending from Nudge’s head. Beyond giving it a unique look, I thought a highly poseable finger could enable different nudging gestures to communicate the priority of notifications. I was also pretty excited by the idea of building a tendon-driven mechanism, partly as a tribute to my time working with humanoid hands at Proception.

However, the feedback was that it looked a little strange, and the complexity didn’t really fit the simplicity we were going for. Adding force sensors, lights, and multiple actuators to a small probe also started to feel impractical.

From Finger to Head Bump

Instead, we moved toward something inspired by how pets interact with people: a gentle head bump.

We still wanted different notification behaviors, from a quick tap to a more persistent nudge. The challenge became expressing those priorities through the body and head, rather than adding more joints to a finger.

The idea was to link the head directly to the leg mechanisms, removing the need for dedicated nudging actuators. Through a differential linkage, independent leg movement would tilt the head in the opposite direction, while moving both legs together would push the head forward to nudge the user.

This meant the head could contribute to Nudge’s personality through passive movement, while still delivering a controlled physical notification. Adding a series elastic element (SEA) would also let us measure contact force through spring deflection, without introducing another actuator.

Finally, we wanted to give Nudge a light-up nose that doubles as an acknowledgment button. Beyond making it visually distinct in a sea of nose-less humanoids, the idea was to combine physical contact, light, movement, and potentially sound to communicate different notification priorities. The nose button was another small nod to how people interact with pets and added haptic feedback to the user experience.

4. Designing the Body

Soft Exterior & Hidden Legs

We knew pretty early on that Nudge was going to be round, soft, and a little bulbous, inspired by characters like Baymax from Big Hero 6 . I wanted it to look approachable enough that someone would instinctively feel comfortable touching or picking it up.

That meant hiding the exposed legs, joints, and linkages commonly found on quadrupeds and wheeled bipeds. In particular, we wanted to eliminate the awkward gap between the wheels and the body.

This led to the idea of embedding the height-adjustment mechanisms inside the wheels themselves , using large, externally mounted rim wheels.

There were a few reasons this made sense. Larger wheels would handle small floor obstacles better, while providing more room to package the mechanisms. They also avoided the enclosed wheel wells of a Roomba-style base, where I was worried debris could accumulate and make maintenance difficult.

There was also a useful mechanical benefit: keeping the actuators in the internal wheel frames meant they could stay low instead of being lifted with the body. That reduced the moving mass, helped keep the center of mass down, and made the height-adjustment job a little easier.

Of course, larger wheels meant more rotating mass and inertia, but they also fit Nudge’s intended shape surprisingly well. At that point, why not make the wheels a major part of the body itself?

A Robot That Isn’t Afraid to Fall

While thinking about round, soft characters, I remembered the punching bag Po uses in Kung Fu Panda , which always rights itself after being knocked over.

That got me thinking: what if Nudge wasn’t afraid to fall over?

Rather than relying entirely on active balancing to keep it safe, we wanted the mechanical design itself to make unexpected contact and tipping less concerning. Three things became especially important:

  • Low kinetic energy: Keep Nudge lightweight and limit its movement speed.
  • Low center of mass: Place as much weight as possible near the ground.
  • Low rotational inertia: Reduce the energy involved when the body tips or rotates.

The embedded-wheel architecture fit nicely into this idea. We could mount the actuators low inside the wheels, keeping their mass close to the ground and avoiding the need to lift them with the body. Combined with a wide stance and soft, rounded exterior, this could make Nudge more resistant to tipping and potentially capable of passively righting itself.

With that, we had arrived at the overall architecture we wanted for Nudge: a small, soft wheeled biped with hidden legs, expressive body movement, and a mechanically linked head for physical notifications.

5. The Leg Mechanism

With Nudge’s overall architecture decided, the next question was how much movement the legs needed and how to achieve it with a compact, practical mechanism.

How Many Degrees of Freedom?

Most wheeled bipeds we looked at used either one or two degrees of freedom (DOF) per leg. I had previously built 2-DOF legs for my quadruped and wheeled biped projects, which allowed the wheels to move both vertically and horizontally relative to the body.

For Nudge, though, that felt unnecessary. We weren’t planning on climbing obstacles, jumping, or doing anything particularly dynamic. Most of its expressive movements would happen while stationary, with the legs mainly responsible for raising, lowering, and tilting the body.

This made a 1-DOF leg much easier to justify. Each leg would independently control its height, allowing Nudge to raise and lower its body or lean from side to side. Turning could be handled through differential wheel speeds, without needing additional leg articulation.

Ultimately, this gave us the movements we wanted with fewer actuators, less weight, and a much simpler mechanical system.

Choosing a Linkage

With the required motion reduced to a single degree of freedom per leg, the next challenge was finding a compact mechanism that could raise and lower the body while fitting inside the wheels.

We considered a few mechanisms, including simple rotating arms, four-bar linkages, and constrained lifting systems using lead screws or belts.

A simple arm was attractive for its simplicity, but would move the body along an arc rather than vertically. A lead screw could hold the body at a fixed height without continuous motor torque, but would make backdrivability and sensing external forces more difficult.

The four-bar linkage seemed like the best compromise. It offered a more controlled body trajectory, relatively simple packaging, and the possibility of adding gravity compensation.

To Spring or Not to Spring?

One idea I kept coming back to was gravity compensation.

Since Nudge’s legs would move relatively slowly, through a small range of motion, and spend plenty of time stationary, it seemed like a particularly good application for a gravity compensation mechanism (GCM) .

The idea was to use a spring to support much of the body’s weight, reducing the continuous torque required from the height actuators. This could let us use smaller motors while keeping the legs easier to move.

The other question was how much compliance and force sensing we actually needed.

There were two main approaches:

  • Quasi-direct drive (QDD): Use a low-reduction, backdrivable actuator and estimate external forces through motor current.
  • Series elastic actuation (SEA): Add a compliant element in series with the actuator and measure its deflection to estimate force.

An SEA would offer more direct force measurement and passive compliance, but also introduce additional components, sensors, and mechanical complexity.

For Nudge, I wasn’t convinced that the legs needed that level of force sensing. We weren’t trying to walk over rough terrain or perform highly dynamic movements, and much of the interaction with the user would happen through the head rather than the wheels.

My preference was to keep the legs as simple as possible: a low-ratio, backdrivable actuator paired with gravity compensation, provided we could find a motor that met the torque requirements.

Bringing It All Together

The nudging mechanism was a slightly different story. Unlike the legs, the head needed to make controlled physical contact with people, so measuring the actual contact force was much more important.

This made a series elastic element at the head easier to justify. We could use the existing leg actuators to generate the nudging motion, while a spring and sensor at the contact point would provide force feedback without adding another motor.

There were still plenty of details to work out, particularly the linkage geometry, spring selection, and how accurately we could estimate forces through the actuators.

Nevertheless, we finally had a direction for Nudge. Questions remained about how the mechanism would work, whether we could fit everything inside the wheels, and how well our ideas would hold up through modeling and prototyping. But for the first time, the different parts of Nudge felt like they were coming together.

The next step was figuring out whether we could actually make it work.
