Robotic Hand

February 2023 — April 2023 • Personal Project

Robotic hands originally inspired me to pursue engineering, so building one felt like the perfect first engineering project: a highly dexterous, anthropomorphic hand.

1. Actuation Architecture

Choosing how to deliver power across linkages heavily impacts both performance and packaging constraints. Early in the design process, I was keen on a cable-driven approach because it most closely emulated human anatomy. Looking at the trade-offs between the three primary robotic hand configurations:

Type Advantages Disadvantages
Direct Drive High torque bandwidth; zero mechanical hysteresis or cable slack; high positional accuracy High distal mass; bulky joints; susceptible to gearbox damage and overload
Tendon Driven Low distal mass and robust (actuators in forearm); supports anthropomorphic finger geometry Friction and creep along routing pathway; nonlinear tendon coupling between joints
Pneumatic Natural mechanical compliance; low system inertia; power-to-weight density Low efficiency; complex pressure regulation; external pumps, valves, and tubing required

Decision: I chose a tendon-driven architecture. Moving the actuators to the forearm allowed the main hand assembly to remain lightweight, simple, and packaged closely to human proportions.

2. Thumb Design

The human thumb accounts for roughly 40% of overall hand utility due to its unique opposability. The thumb, and by extension the fingers, suffer from a challenging trade-off between high posability (degrees of freedom) and controllability. Packaging constraints further complicate the design.

2.1 CMC Joint Architecture

The thumb’s opposability primarily originates from the carpometacarpal (CMC) joint located at the base of the thumb. Anatomically, the CMC is a saddle joint capable of multi-axis motion. To simplify the mechanical design, I modelled the joint as a 2-DOF universal joint (u-joint), inspired by a nested camera gimbal.

This preserved thumb opposability while reducing mechanical complexity.

2.2 Tendon Actuation

Four cables were dedicated to driving the u-joint itself. Each degree of freedom was controlled antagonistically using paired cables routed to opposite sides of the joint. Knots were used as terminations and cutouts were made to let the tendons swing freely without interference or binding.

2.3 Kinematic Decoupling Strategy

The principal challenge was routing an additional independent tendon required to actuate the thumb’s distal tip without coupling it to the base joint’s movements. I solved this by routing the distal tendon through the concentric center of rotation of the universal joint. This minimized changes in tendon length regardless of base orientation, reducing the need for additional software compensation or active control strategies.

Although the distal tendon was decoupled, I couldn’t find a great decoupling strategy for the tendons controlling the u-joint itself before the end of my internship. I settled on the same axis of rotation bending technique used on the center running tendon.

The two tendons controlling the outer ring would remain taut throughout articulation, while the two tendons driving the inner ring would bend about the outer ring’s axis of rotation before entering the palm. I explored several alternative concepts, including a spherical gear design intended to eliminate the swinging motion of the two inner tendons, though none proved successful.

In retrospect, a more robust solution would likely have involved mechanically separating the two degrees of freedom and/or incorporating rollers or pulley surfaces to better manage tendon wrap throughout the thumb’s range of motion.

3. Underactuated Dynamics

Three approaches were considered for finger flexion and extension:

Type Advantages Disadvantages
Passive Elastic Extension Reduced actuator and tendon count; naturally compliant, tolerant to positioning error Reduced extension authority; non-linear restoring force; limited positional control
Dedicated Flexion + Extension Full control; deterministic joint positioning Increased actuator count; poor passive compliance without force control
Bidirectional Tendon Single actuator controls both flexion and extension; compact Tendon slack and tensioning issues; reduced compliance; sensitive to cable creep and backlash

Decision: I chose a passive elastic extension architecture. This reduced actuator and tendon count while enabling naturally compliant grasping behaviour.

The primary trade-offs were reduced extension authority, lower positional controllability, and a non-linear restoring force throughout the finger’s range of motion. However, given the inexpensive hobby servos I was prototyping with, which were operating through PWM signals with no externally available force sensing or feedback, passive extension was the most practical architecture capable of compliant grasping (discounting more complex solutions like SEAs). Tendons were terminated using embedded knots within the finger components. Extension cables were secured using fasteners on the back of the fingers.

Sequential Joint Flexion

I configured the tendon routing geometry and linkage proportions within the fingers to produce a non-uniform mechanical advantage across the joints. During flexion, the proximal joint would rotate first under tendon tension before progressively transferring torque to the medial and distal joints. This underactuated behaviour allowed the fingers to naturally wrap and conform around arbitrary object geometries during grasping.

In practice, tuning this behaviour was challenging due to uncertain elastic restoring forces, friction losses and variability within the tendon routing. Using one tendon to drive all three joints significantly simplified the mechanism but also reduced controllability and made the flexion sequence highly sensitive to small changes in geometry and system friction.

4. System Integration

The palm and the forearm were built as simple routing pieces for the tendons and mounting for the servos. The control stack consisted of a Raspberry Pi commanding seven hobby servos pulling nine tendons through a PCA9685 PWM driver board, allowing for coordinated, multi-DOF grasping sequences.

5. Retrospective and Lessons Learned

Looking back, this project was a success as a first engineering project with a goal to build a functioning, posable hand that matched human proportions. However, there is a long list of things I would do differently today:

  • Tendon Material and Terminations: Using thin fishing line made routing easy, but it was incredibly frustrating to tie off. The knots would slowly slip under tension, changing the finger calibration over time.
  • Passive Extension Element: The elastic bands used fatigued quickly and their extension force was unknown. It was also difficult to consistently terminate them.
  • Actuator Limits: The cheap hobby servos were tough to work with. Operating on open-loop PWM signals with zero force feedback meant I couldn't command a specific grip strength; I just had to drive the motor until the passive mechanics did the wrapping.
  • Limited Degrees of Freedom: Driving each finger with a single tendon cable restricted overall posability. Adding independent tendons per joint would enable individual joint articulation and support active abduction/adduction.

Plus, many others. I learned a lot about cable-driven systems from this project that I brought forward with me to future challenges and experiences. A lot of that can be seen in the work I did at Proception AI.
